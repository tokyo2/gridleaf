Best Paper for Printing Planners at Home

The paper matters more than the printer. A heavier, smoother sheet stops gel pens from showing through, survives a year of page-turning and makes a home-printed planner feel like a bought one. Here's how to choose, with every US paper weight converted to grams.

The short answer

  • Most planner pages: 24 lb (90 gsm) bright white paper. It costs a little more than copy paper, feels noticeably better and feeds in almost every home printer.
  • Double-sided pages, gel pens or highlighters: 28 to 32 lb (105–120 gsm) paper, which is much more opaque.
  • Covers, dividers and fridge calendars: 65 lb cover card stock (176 gsm), or 80 lb (216 gsm) if your printer can feed it.

Paper weights explained

US paper weights are confusing because the number in pounds depends on the type of paper. “Bond” (copy paper), “cover” (card stock), “text” and “index” are each weighed at a different basis size, so a 70 lb text paper (about 104 gsm) is roughly the same as 28 lb bond (about 105 gsm). Grams per square meter (gsm) is measured the same way for every paper, which makes it the number to compare.

Bleed-through and ghosting

Ghosting is when you can see writing from the other side of the page; bleed-through is when ink actually soaks through. Both are mostly about paper weight and opacity, so the fixes are simple:

  • Use 28–32 lb paper for anything printed on both sides.
  • Choose fine-tip pens with quick-drying ink, and test new pens on the notes page at the back first.
  • Press lightly with highlighters. Mild or pastel highlighters hide less of your writing.
  • Print the pages you write on most, like daily pages, on one side only.

Brightness, finish and color

Brightness is the number on the pack, usually between the low 90s and 100. Higher numbers look whiter and make thin lines and small text crisper. Finish matters more than people expect: smooth, uncoated paper is best for writing. Avoid glossy photo paper for planner pages, because ballpoints skip and gel ink smears on it. Ivory or cream paper is easier on the eyes and suits soft, neutral designs, but colors print a little warmer.

Inkjet or laser printer?

Both print planners well. Inkjet printers handle the widest range of papers, including matte coated paper; let heavy pages dry for a minute before printing the back. Laser printers fuse toner with heat: ordinary copy paper and laser-rated papers are fine, but never put inkjet-only coated or photo paper through them. Whichever you have, look up its maximum paper weight (usually given in gsm) in the manual. Many home printers feed card stock best from the rear or manual tray, one sheet at a time.

Letter or A4?

US Letter is 8.5 × 11 inches (215.9 × 279.4 mm) and A4 is 210 × 297 mm (8.27 × 11.69 inches). Print on the size the file was made for: an A4 page printed on Letter paper has to shrink to about 94% to fit, and a Letter page on A4 to about 97%, which makes boxes and lines slightly smaller. Planner paper FAQ

What paper weight is best for a printable planner?

24 lb (90 gsm) paper for pages printed on one side, and 28 to 32 lb (105–120 gsm) for double-sided pages or if you write with gel pens.

What's the difference between 32 lb paper and 80 lb card stock?

They are measured on different scales. 32 lb bond paper is about 120 gsm, while 80 lb cover card stock is about 216 gsm, almost twice as heavy. That's why grams per square meter (gsm) is the easier number to compare.

Can I print every planner page on card stock?

You can, but a whole planner on card stock gets thick and stiff, and many home printers only feed card one sheet at a time. Use card stock for covers, dividers and pages that hang on the wall.

Does paper brightness matter?

A little. Brighter paper gives crisper contrast for text and thin lines. Ivory or cream paper is softer on the eyes and makes colors print slightly warmer.

Should I print on Letter or A4?

Letter for the US and Canada, A4 for most other countries. Every Gridleaf printable comes in both sizes, so print at 100% (Actual size) on matching paper.
